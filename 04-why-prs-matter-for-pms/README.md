# Why should a PM care about pull requests?

Because the pull request is where a change to your product actually gets decided, checked and approved before customers see it. If you can read one, you can see what's really shipping, catch problems while they're cheap to fix, and leave a record of why every decision was made.

![Level: Beginner](https://img.shields.io/badge/Level-Beginner-brightgreen) ![Type: Learn and Build](https://img.shields.io/badge/Type-Learn%20%2B%20Build-blue) ![Time: 65 min](https://img.shields.io/badge/Time-65%20min-orange) ![Last checked: 2026-11](https://img.shields.io/badge/Last%20checked-2026--11-lightgrey)

> **TL;DR** The ticket says what you want. The pull request (PR) is where the real number, the real wording and the edge cases get settled. You don't need to read every line of code to review one. Read the title, the description, the list of what changed, the checks and the conversation, and ask whether they all tell the same story. Then write your own PRs so they work as your paper trail: an outcome title, a short description, and a link back to the ticket.

> [!TIP]
> **New to this? Let me break it down.**
> Picture the Polish & Co. policy binder at the front desk. The manager wants to change the cancellation rule. She doesn't just cross out a line. She writes the new wording on a sticky note, adds a line about why, and leaves it for the owner. The owner reads it, asks "what about people who already booked?", and only then does it go in the binder. The note goes in a folder, so six months later anyone can see what changed, why, and who said yes.
>
> A PR is that sticky note, plus the conversation, plus the folder. As a PM, you're often the owner reading the note.
